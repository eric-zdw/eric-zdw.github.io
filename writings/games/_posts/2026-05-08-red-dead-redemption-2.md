6.5 (Recommended)

My recollection of playing *Red Dead Redemption 2* is one of two distinct impressions, different to the point of belonging to two different games: one is linear, messy, violent, sometimes brutal but mostly senseless, the gameplay and characters unable to support the greater narrative; the other is relaxed, grounded in detail, a framework upon which to observe a painstakingly detailed world; its preoccupations of leaving behind artifacts and being remembrance manifesting in one of the most detailed depictions of a vertical slice in time.

The former impression, the campaign, follows Arthur Morgan and his gradual dillusionment with the Van Der Linde Gang through several discrete missions. Most missions start and end by travelling to a destination with a companion, leaving plenty of space for observing the environment and dialogue. All of Rockstar's open world missions hinge on this travelling between destinations and building of rapport in the downtime, and in these moments Rockstar have always recognized that their environments are their most memorable characters, making the surroundings either the point of focus or setting the emotional stage. The narrative's arc can be traced in the order of locales visited: winter giving way to crisp spring from Chapter 1 to 2; moral decay made manifest in Rhodes' red dust and sweltering temperatures; the forest shades a respite at the crossroads in Chapter 6. It's telling that this time around, Rockstar have added a "Cinematic Camera", which I would argue has very little to do with cinematic technique beyond the letterboxing: what it really does is decouple the camera from the player, framing characters against their surroundings; it keeps the player character's action at a physical and emotional distance while bringing the character of the environments into clear view.

However, it's the dialogue during these moments that continue to be Rockstar's most deeply felt missed opportunities. The dialogue that usually ensues is a rehashing of events that the player has *just* experienced, or a flat recitation of what the gang's next plans are. Moments that truly expand a character's dimensions beyond the way they talk are rare, and moments that expand the narrative outside the bounds of the game itself are virtually non-existent. Despite the narrative hinging upon the closeness of its main cast, it's hard to recall any moment in RDR2 between two characters that resembles full-bodied dialogue, a conversation between two friends who could sustain each other for hours in each other's company; even in camp, most conversations are over in a matter of statements, often exchanges of empty platitudes. These games could be far improved by a careful examination of where these narrative moments can be used in combination with their detailed environments to create truly resonant moments that are more than the sum of their parts.

The action gameplay doesn't add much to the equation; it's neither interesting in its own right, nor does it help convey the greater narrative. Much of the action takes the form of shooting gallery sequences, which don't capture the high-stakes life-or-death engagements the western genre is known for. The odds are simply skewed far into the player's favor, who has the vitality of an action hero, and any injuries can be undone with the overabundance of healing items the player will accumulate over the course of playing, leaving the impact of a bullet mostly toothless. Even for the most inexperienced players, the visual of being shot several times from all sides, blood dripping all over Arthur's clothes, only to shake them off with a bottle of elixir must have some detrimental effects to the life-or-death engagements these missions constantly want to sell the image of.

It's not a system built for visceral, dramatic combat, but it's also not built for deeper decision-making. All guns function effectively the same as each other with minor variations in damage, accuracy and ammo capacity. All guns are capable of killing instantly with a single headshot, with the degenerate consequences of a) evening the damage playing field of all guns; b) centralizing the gameplay overly towards Dead Eye, which ensures you can hit a headshot on any and all targets at will. The low-powered revolver obsoletes nearly all other guns due to its draw speed, its inaccuracy and damage virtually a non-factor. As a silver lining, because the game never actually asks for a high degree of mastery and optimization beyond optional mission medals, the decisions in weaponry really came down to which gun I aesthetically preferred to have in my hands--the Springfield rifle was my mainstay, with its crisp sound and single-bullet capacity best complimenting precision shots to the head.

Meaningfully scripted encounter design is rare. Missions are tightly scripted in an explicitly narrative sense, but not in ways that shake up the moment-to-moment shooting in novel ways. It's surprising that all enemies are vulnerable to headshots with no exception, and given that such an obvious enemy weakness must be an intentional decision, it makes clear that, more than anything, the priority is not so much creating a system where players must think about their gameplay, it's more to make sure players can ride out any gunfight smoothly and without hastle. The occassional train robberies are the encounters at their best; the narrows corridors add wrinkles to the pattern, restricting the range of motion to a central push to the front and turning some exchanges into close-range twitch reactions of the shotgun; on the other hand, the most egregious setpieces have you ducking under cover in front of a building and balcony, shooting enemy after enemy as they pop out of the windows and doors, the moment-to-moment gameplay more reminiscent of an 90s shooting gallery than a serious thriller.

(Honorable mentions go to the music during combat: it makes the most of a thin storyline or moments of meaningless violent action and provides a gravitational backdrop that hints at some of the grander scale that otherwise goes uncovered. The modern instrumentation of the score evokes a revisionist flare and sometimes feels as though historical fiction is rising from time.)

Combat, killing, looting the occassional body, watching a cutscene of the same close calls--the whole loop becomes monotonous, smoothed-over within the first twenty hours of the game. (A major contributor to this feeling is that very little control is ceded to the player, who is constantly being railroading from one setpiece to the next cutscene to the next objective.) The occasional slow-motion kill, meant to punctuate the action but common enough to appear a handful of times in a single mission, itself becomes a part of the monotony. Even worse, the narrative experience encapsulated with the missions itself becomes monotonous, smoothed-over. More than any other feeling, my memories of playing through the missions in RDR2 seem to be characterized by a haze of senseless violence that, in a perverse way, occassionally complements the senselessness and nihilistic undercurrent of the narrative, but also injects a sense of boredom and listlessness, two feelings that I think are at odds with the overall objective here.

The way individual missions appear to slot into the overall narrative contributes to the problem. For the majority of the game, the story appears to be structured as follows: The first handful of missions in the chapter establishes the new setting, as the gang settles in; the last handful recount the events that force the gang to leave; every mission in between is its own self-enclosed shootout, not entirely relevant to the narrative to the extent that they're interchangable within the same chapter. Each mission needs to leave the overarching plan intact for the rest of the missions to continue functioning (indeed, missions are largely interchangable between chapters, being able to select from a number of missions at any given time). It's not an inherently empty strategy, but each mission in itself is just so inconsequential; few situations offer any meaningful direction for self-reflection. The result is a narrative with the structure of a novel, whose pages seem filled with text but is empty in meaningful narrative movement.

Characters that the narrative revolve around suffer in characterization perhaps as a result. A great deal of the narrative's greater meaning hinges on Dutch's personal downfall, but he's never a convincingly charismatic leader with any sense of smart thinking. The emotional ambiguity behind his actions give off a strong sense that things simply happen because the writers decide they need to happen, not from a careful examination of cause-and-effect of his underlying character traits. Micah never takes off as a character grounded to this world's reality, in part due to a fatally paced mission where he murders an entire town to retrieve his guns, with no repercussions by the gang (I don't think the incident is ever mentioned in conversation after the mission). Sadie's narrative never rises above its premises, it never develops into a meaningful exploration on the nature of revenge: the story's only motivation is that she gets it.

The ending of Arthur's story is mostly incomprehensible, his impending death and reckoning of his past tied to one of the most reductive morality systems in recent memory--one where saying hello to five people offsets killing someone in cold blood-- culminating in a moral fist-fight to the death of towering proportions against Micah, a criminal for the *wrong* reasons, or at least not whichever reasons you decide to make them in your own playthrough. Besides the obvious issues of reducing morality, a human phenomenon as complex as the world, to a binary scale, Rockstar's choice to have the honor system influence the narrative is also fatal: the consequence of accomodating both high-honor and low-honor narratives is that the narrative ends up acknowledging neither outcome. The only changes are superficial (which spirit animal will *you* see when you die?), and any meaningful differences are erased once the epilogue begins: neither moral outcome results in any meaningful difference in John's trajectory. (You could argue that the intention was to show the amorality of the world at large, uncaring beyond death; the problem includes the fact that the system inadvertently shows the meaninglessness of the system during life).

The Western genre is brimming with so many stories across so many mediums that the narrative in RDR2 begs comparison. But the narrative is nothing we haven't already seen in a revisionist western, with more precision and pathos. "Unchanged man in a changing land", reads the poster tagline for *The Wild Bunch*, a film that approaches very similar themes but with arguably more poignancy, sensitivity and passionate urgency. (For a game that claims to achieve a parity with cinema, there's never a moment as cinematically expressive as the sheer violence, brutality and confusion of cause-and-effect in *Wild Bunch*'s first quick-cut meditation of a bank shootout.) It's arguable that the emptiest moments of the game stem from this desire to emulate cinema, not recognizing that paying homage to the Western might be better served by playing to the current strengths of the medium at hand. RDR2 has the advantage of a long runtime to portray a more encompassing, novelistic depiction of the death of the Wild West, but the end result here suffers from long stretches where there simply isn't much to be said--at least, not in its characters or its words.

Red Dead Redemption 2 is a game characterized by its messy main storyline, and it's impossible to avoid interfacing with this side of the game. But with such missed potential, it's surprising that the final impression is still a game with much to see, and time spent immersed in carefully built environments.

Most immediately striking is how the player is made to traverse the world. The detail and general sluggishness in which Arthur moves, via walking, running, defines how the game is felt when playing, and it's fascinating just how reliant the game is on this (think how different the game would register on an emotional level if characters ran at typical game speeds, or imagine the feet-slipping of older games.)

Survival appears to play a key role in the characterization of the player's relationship with the world; the player character lives within the world and its rules. But survival becomes trivial once you have a grasp on the mechanics that drive it. While it's fascinating that the system seems carefully balanced such that it's always present without ever truly interfering with the game, as it stands, the color that the system provides to the final experience is a bit faint and inconsequential, and you could look to several examples of similar open-world experiences with a similar level of interfacing with the world without having a needs system.

More importantly, the survival system is a stand-in to push the player into interacting with the world at large.

The points of interest in RDR2 often have some level of unpredictable intrigue to them. Each point of interest has some sort of narrative thread to follow, sometimes an in-narrative mystery or tragedy, sometimes a reference to real-life history. In that way, Red Dead Redemption 2 is more successful that the average open world game at capturing the sense that there are things in the world to be discovered (compare to Breath of the Wild, where a shrine acts as the travelling equivalent of an overseas McDonald's: you might find a new limited time marble maze variation, but after the first few visits you know exactly what you're going to get when you take the elevator, no matter which country you happen to be in).

The method in which Rockstar goads the player into engaging with the world they've built might be outdated, and often adds a level of degenerate attitude towards the motivations of the player. It's not a total immersion-breaker but a seperation occurs on some level between Arthur the player and Arthur the character when the player is wreaking havoc on the local wildlife trying to complete the 8th Master Hunter challenge, in a way that is admittedly quite funny but perhaps undermines some of the effort put into making so much of the moveset grounded, so much effort put into making this character a product of the world.

There is a sense of the need to create something that will last, an acute awareness of the passage of time, and this preoccupation bleeds out of the main narrative into the overall gameplay experience. For example: what is in the value of the environments that was worth spending so many resources, so many hours spent on advisors? Why is it important that rivers appear to follow real-world rules of formation? The fidelity crosses any reasonable threshold--many details likely will never be discovered by the average player or the collective public. It's not an attempt to create a real snapshot of the times--there are way too many concessions towards gameplay, the scale of the world is too condensed. The most fascinating aspect of this game is a sense of fidelity for fidelity's sake, the belief that the sheer detail poured into an area is sufficient to create something with meaning, or if not meaning, then memorability. It's in this motivation that I feel most acutely in the epilogue areas, vast swaths of desert with very little "gameplay" content but otherwise fully detailed, fully traversable. 

The game's fidelity is a very specific one, one that lends to its world a sense of stasis within a small, specific "slice" in time. Consider the game's depictions of seasons--instead of changing across the world, seasons are limited to specific biomes. Time ostensibly passes in the form of a day/night cycle, and there is movement and activity, but it is always the *same* cycle, *same* movement and activity: there will always be specific types of birds in the sky in this area; there will always be a certain density of people at a given time of day in a given town. This has always been true for past Rockstar games, where hardware limitations of the times made more complex depictions infeasible. But now, the focus on such a static fidelity changes the presentation of these aspects in a way that impacts the experience differently, increasingly a limitation of choice over neccessity. Now they are not just details that fill out a living world, they are also elements that strike me as static in a world whose visual fidelity has surpassed its temporal fidelity. And whether by strict intention or by accident, the result is in a way uniquely authorial, it expresses a specific, personal idea on what it means to be high-fidelity: what matters is the detailed image frozen in time, like John's sepia photograph.

The ultimate payoff, when all of these elements converge, can be breathtaking. My memorable moments of the game come from the immersion that comes from the intersection of intently exploring the world and the backdrop of weather effects: traversing the top of Mount Shann, taking care not to fall off the narrow path; watching the morning sun rays shine through the slivers between the trees, the fog illuminated so brightly by the sun that you can't see further than 10 meters.
