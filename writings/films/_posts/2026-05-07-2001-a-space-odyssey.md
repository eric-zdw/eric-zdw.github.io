---
title: "2001: A Space Odyssey"
---

\*\*\*\*\*

The film starts with "people, but not yet". We're asked to observe from a distance. Cutting to the distant future so that we can extend that observational attitude to the rest that follows. It lets us notice that, even in this version of the far flung future, people still behave as if they're in the 60s, technologies are antiquated in a quaint way (as of the time of this writing, 2026). Surely Kubrick was aware these mannerisms would seem out of place in a future sci-fi setting--he didn't make these "mistakes" in other period films like Paths of Glory, or Barry Lyndon--it's more likely that he felt using the current mannerisms of the times would serve the point of the film better. It serves two different audiences at once: the contemporary audience, who can observe their own behaviours from a vantage point, and the audience of the future, who can observe the behaviours and sentiments of an era that has already passed.

The use of specific pieces in the soundtrack allows for introspection of its own function as well, as pieces as cultural artifacts. At its core, two objects float and approach each other, and it looks like a dance once waltz music plays on top of it. Without the Blue Danube, does the docking spaceship scene still resemble a dance? The Blue Danube and Also sprach Zarathustra are audibly "songs"--does Ligeti's Atmospheres also have the function of a "song", or does the audience accept its use more "naturally" as background music because it doesn't sound familiarly like how a classical piece should? Through the whole runtime, this film is taking the facts we take for granted of how we interpret the world, revealing them, and setting aside time to reflect.

The monolith: what makes it interesting is that it doesn't effectively function as a symbol: its meaning doesn't rely on abstract symbolism or context (in fact you *cannot* bring outside context into its meaning, it being so simple, plain, of singular form). Here the "unknowable" is synonymous with "uncontextualizable", but it can be understood purely by its effects on the screen. Flashes of inspiration from its striking image, by the way its shape and texture is unlike its environment, by the way the apeman recalls in a moment it towering above and pointing to the sun in the sky. A simple idea: faced with the unknown and the different, the natural human response is to ponder, wonder at our conditions. Here it will inspire a human to think to use tools and set off a chain reaction that implies the events of all recorded human history.

Dave and HAL are engaged in one-on-one conversation at all times. Near the end of the HAL sequence is the first time that communication breaks: combined with the disembodiment of HAL's voice, the effect is such that HAL's dialogue registers to us as internal monologue, even though in-universe his voice is being played omnisciently on the ship intercom. Some will automatically accept HAL as a villain, some will consider HAL the most "human" character of the sequence; the important part is that Kubrick understood that the most interesting decision is not to take sides with sentimental movements of the camera or editing, but to let the audience observe, take the time to come to their own terms with the experience.

The stargate sequence: maybe my biggest gripe with the film is in this section. Visually the film seems to play with the basics of color and shape (changes in color can make Monument Valley look alien and barren, the orange sky and landscape in shadow can make it look inhospitably on fire) but the audible effect is far from minimal. Abstract images of the cosmos are accompanied by a low rumbling and orchestral swells. This strikes me as a bit of cheating, the expression coming from the image being supplemented by sentimental sound choice (even worse: the image already evokes forces on a massive scale as the music implies. It could be silent and the effect would be totally intact). It seems contradictory that the film is willing to expose the audience to minutes of a blank screen accompanied by music, but reluctant to let visuals speak for themselves at crucial moments.

At the end of the world is a chance to put anything. The end of comprehension is the domain of the creator's imagination--it is their chance to put what matters to them front and center. In Outer Wilds, taking this film as direct inspiration, the edge of the world takes the form of a totally artificial creation: a museum of everything the player has learned about the world before it ceases to exist, a shrine to celebrate pursuing knowledge and experience for its own sake. Here, the edge of the world is an interior full of "human artifacts": decorated walls, antiquated paintings and sculpture, tables and chairs, drawers, a bed. Time and perspective lose their continuity. The camera is placed in areas that imply impossible, simultaneous first-person (experience) and third-person (observer) points of view of a single person. One final act of observation: this time we observe the fundamental trajectory of a human eating, growing old, dying, pointing at the unknown (is reaching for the unknown as fundamental as eating and sleeping?). Then he is unexpectedly "reborn". It's fascinating that to Kubrick, the ascension to a higher form of life looks like *re*-birth, being young *again*, *regression* in time. What emotions does a baby evoke? When I think of a baby in real life they seem to react with wonder, blankness, sometimes absolute terror. A baby has no understanding of the world, but when a baby stares at something, it stares in captivation, total immersion in the mystery of what is. In contradiction, this baby doesn't seem to show a different captivation. Not "blankness", but intensity in its gaze, awareness in its eye, lucidly observing the Earth on the opposite end of the screen.
