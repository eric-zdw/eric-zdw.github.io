---
title: Le Trou
medium: films
---

Unusually for a prison escape film, the danger of escape in *Le Trou* never quite materializes. Early on, a four-minute uncut process of three inmates cracking a hole through the floor obliterates any suspension of disbelief--surely the guards can hear the digging and realize it's not the sound of folding cardboard. In general, the guards are so sloppy at their jobs, some near misses so comical that it seems the inmates could get away with murder. It's hard to respect the process playing out on the screen if there are no stakes, and even early on there's a sense that any opposition that plays out won't be from the escape attempt but from the relationship between the inmates.

Like the prison escape itself, the spirit of the film seems to be less about precision, more DIY, impulse and a little sloppiness: the film is ready to obsessively show the entire uncut process of digging the first hole, as if to prove a point in the effort required, but also willing to skip entire portions of getting to and digging through the sewer wall for the sake of brevity (evading guards must be too boring or besides the point). The film is happy to repeat three matching shots of walking towards the vanishing point into a dark hallway, without much greater purpose than to show it three times. The toothbrush mirror is maybe too clever a camera trick for its own good, pulling me out of immersion and leaving me wondering when the film would release its Dramatic Mirror Payoff (surprise! at the very end).

Not to say there aren't moments of poignancy and motion: there's an excitement in watching Manu break through the final wall fragment by fragment by fragment, the camera flipping sides, letting us wait patiently on the opposite end. A general understatement of emotion welled up until this moment allows the first-person glimpse of the peaceful outdoors in early morning, dusted with snow, otherwise unremarkable but quietly inviting. And when they must instead return to the prison, it builds an emotional pressure that brings Gaspard's sense of bourgeois alienation to the front and sets the ending into motion.

But while the ending is a cathartic release of energy and action, it also collapses the possibilities of the film into a simplistic moral parable that strikes me as neither insightful nor convincing. We can only accept the film's moral condemnation of Gaspard if we ignore the circumstances of the inmates' crimes, we decide that solitary and execution (but with friends!) is a meaningful moral victory compared to being released or getting a sentence reduction (but corruptly!), and we assume Gaspard's decision precludes any future redemption, all of which are major oversimplifications. The film's final moral statement can only be accepted on its own formalist terms, detatched from the world beyond the prison walls.
